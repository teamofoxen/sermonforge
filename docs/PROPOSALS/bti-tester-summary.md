# SermonForge Beta — A Note for the Cohort

A short letter to the pastors I'm asking to test SermonForge with me.

---

## What this is

I've been building SermonForge for a while now. It's a sermon prep tool, designed around one stubborn conviction: a system can deepen the pastor's work by asking the right questions, in the right order, at the right time — and forcing the pastor to do the answering. The system asks; you answer. That's the deal.

I started this project assuming AI would be part of that — a partner that could help where help was wanted. After a year of building, I came to the opposite conclusion: any AI surface in this kind of tool ends up doing some of the work the pastor was supposed to do, even when it's polite about it. So I removed all of it. SermonForge is now end-to-end pastor-authored. The system forces clarity by asking; you write every word that lands in your sermon.

That conviction is the whole point. If it doesn't hold up in real prep, in real weeks, with real pastors, then SermonForge isn't worth shipping.

I can't test that by myself. I built it. I know what the questions are *meant* to do, which means I can't feel what it's actually like to walk through them on a Wednesday morning when I have a passage I'm wrestling with and a Sunday coming. I need a small group of pastors I trust to use it on real sermons over real weeks and tell me the truth about what they find.

That's what this is. You're one of about twenty pastors I'm asking. You all know me. That's the point — and it's also the catch.

---

## The hard ask

Here's the thing I need from you, and it's harder than it sounds because we're friends.

**I need you to be the kind of friend who will tell me the tool is in your way.**

Not the kind of friend who tells me it's promising, or that it's coming along, or that they can see what I'm going for. I have plenty of those instincts in myself already. What I cannot get from inside my own head is the moment a pastor opens the app on a Wednesday morning, sees what's in front of them, and either thinks *"this is helping me think"* or thinks *"this is in my way"* — and goes back to their old workflow without telling me.

The way SermonForge dies is not a bug report. It's pastors quietly drifting back to what they were doing before. Or pastors using it but feeling, after a few weeks, that the sermons coming out of it don't sound quite like them anymore — that the system's questions have started shaping their answers in ways they didn't choose.

So the things I most need to hear are:

- "I tried it twice this week and went back to Logos."
- "I keep meaning to open it and I forget."
- "I wrote the synthesis answer in fifteen seconds and when I read it back I'm not sure that's what I actually think — it's just the sentence the question pulled out of me."
- "When I read back through this sermon I'm not sure how much of it is actually mine and how much is the shape the questions imposed."
- "I don't trust it enough to put a real upcoming sermon in it."

Those are the comments that save the project. The encouragement comments are kind, and I'll receive them, but they won't change a single decision I make. The hard ones will.

If you can't give me that kind of feedback, that's completely fine — please tell me, and we'll find another way for you to help. But please don't sign up for this and then politely soft-pedal. That's the failure mode this whole program is designed to prevent.

---

## What you're signing up for

A few months of using SermonForge for real sermon prep — your normal weekly preaching, not a special test passage. You'll be using the regular app — the same build anyone downloading SermonForge today gets — with the feedback surfaces built into it. I'll send you the download link and a short setup note.

There are three ways to give feedback, designed for three different amounts of time:

**A flag button — thirty seconds.** Inside the app, on each tab where you're authoring something — Study, Blueprint — there's a small flag button. If something feels off in the moment — a question put a word in your mouth, the flow nudged you somewhere you wouldn't have gone, a field felt heavier than it should — click it. You can leave a one-line note or just click and move on. Even a flag with no note tells me where to look. Use it freely. I'd rather get a hundred half-formed flags than ten polished ones.

**A longer feedback form — five minutes.** A pop-out you can open from the sidebar when you have five minutes and want to say more. A handful of named dimensions (structural overreach, workflow-fit, question quality, trust, friction, voice and frame, and a few others), free-text answers, fill in what you have time for. Good for the things that don't fit a flag — patterns you noticed across the week, things that have been bothering you but you couldn't put your finger on.

**A check-in conversation — thirty minutes, every few weeks.** Async written exchange, voice memo, Loom recording, or a phone call — whatever's easiest for you. I'll send you a short prompt; you respond when you can. The questions will lean concrete: *did you open the app this week without me asking? When you didn't, what were you doing instead? Read your last sub-phase synthesis answer back to yourself — is that the sentence you would have written if I'd asked you the same question on a blank page?* These are the most valuable conversations of the whole program, but they're also the biggest time ask, so they don't happen every week.

## What I'm capturing in the background

Because I trust you and you trust me, I'm planning to capture some technical signals about how you use the app — how often you open it, which tabs hold your attention, where the tour drops you off, whether sermons get started and finished or abandoned mid-prep. This is the part of the program where I can ask for more than I'd ever ask of a public user.

It all goes to me, on systems I control. No third-party analytics, no public dashboards. **Sermon content is never captured** — not your manuscript, not your outline, not your notebook, nothing you typed into any field. Only metadata about your interactions and whatever you choose to send via flags or forms. A separate short doc explains exactly what's captured and what isn't — please read it before you install. If anything in it feels uncomfortable, tell me and we'll change it.

---

## An optional deeper ask

There's one harder thing I'm asking *only* if you want to give it. It's optional and a real burden, and skipping it doesn't make you a lesser tester.

The risk I'm most worried about is what I'm calling *frame drift*: the system's question structure slowly bleeding into your reflexes over weeks of use. Even though every word you type is yours, a question flow can shape which moves come naturally — which structural arc feels right, which theological emphases come up first, which vocabulary the system's grammar most readily admits. The thing that's hardest to catch about this is that by the time it's happening, it feels like your own thinking. You can't really self-report it.

So if you're up for it, I'd ask you to do three short writing exercises during the program — one before you install the app, one in the middle, one at the end. Fifteen to thirty minutes each, on a short passage I'll give you, no tools, just you and the text. I'll read all three together at the end and look for shifts in shape — vocabulary, structural moves, theological reflexes — not in quality.

If you opt in, this is the deepest help you can give me. If you don't, I'll lean on the rest of the feedback for the same signal, and that's fine.

## What you'll see back from me

Every couple of weeks I'll send the cohort a short digest:

- What testers flagged this round
- What landed in the app because of it
- What's in the queue
- What I decided not to do, and why

The point is that you can see your feedback actually moving the tool. If you flag something and it disappears into a void, that's a failure of mine — not a reason for you to stop flagging. The digest is the antidote to that.

I'll also reach out personally if I notice you've gone quiet for a couple of weeks. It won't be a metrics check-in — just *"hey, haven't seen you in the app, is it the tool or just life?"* Either answer is fine. If it's life, no problem at all, no pressure to come back. If it's the tool, that conversation is itself one of the most valuable things you can give me — pastors who quietly stopped using a tool tell me more about what's wrong than pastors who keep using it.

If you want to step out of the cohort entirely at any point, just say so. No explanation needed. You're not letting me down.

---

## What's not your job

Some things I want to take off your plate explicitly:

- **You don't need to take notes.** The flag button captures the moment. The form captures the week. The check-in captures the season. You don't have to remember anything between them.
- **You don't need to file bug reports.** If something looks broken, just flag it and move on. I'll figure out what it is.
- **You don't need to be a careful tester.** Use it for real prep, the way you'd use any tool. The signal I want is what an actual pastor's actual week looks like with this thing in it — not a clean test pass.
- **You don't need to be encouraging.** I have my wife, my elders, and my own stubbornness for that. I need you for something different.

---

## Getting started

When you're ready:

1. Read the short "what we capture" doc I'll send alongside this one.
2. Tell me you're in (or not — both fine).
3. If you opted in for the writing exercises, I'll send you the first short passage to draft against, *before* you install the app.
4. Install the app, set it up, take a look around. There'll be a short tour.
5. Use it for your normal sermon prep. Flag what you notice. Send a longer form when you have something to say. We'll do our first check-in conversation a few weeks in.

If anything in this letter doesn't sit right — the asks, the data, the writing exercises, the time commitment — please push back before you commit. I'd rather lose a tester at this stage than have you in the cohort with reservations.

Thank you for considering this. The fact that you might say yes is itself a gift. I know what I'm asking.
